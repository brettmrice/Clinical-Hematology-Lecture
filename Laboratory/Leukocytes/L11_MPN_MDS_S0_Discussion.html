Laboratory Investigation - MPNs and MDSs

Learning Objectives

  • Differentiate the fundamental pathophysiological mechanisms distinguishing Myeloproliferative Neoplasms (effective, uncontrolled clonal proliferation) from Myelodysplastic Syndromes (ineffective clonal hematopoiesis and intramedullary apoptosis).
  • Evaluate automated Complete Blood Count with Differential (CBCD) parameters and flag patterns to identify classic Myeloproliferative Neoplasms (CML, PV, ET, PMF) and correlate them with diagnostic peripheral blood smear morphological findings.
  • Identify and classify peripheral blood morphologic dysplasia across erythroid, granulocytic, and megakaryocytic lineages in patients presenting with unexplained, persistent cytopenias.
  • Formulate bone marrow examination protocols and benchtop cytochemical/histological procedures (LAP scoring, Perls' Prussian blue, reticulin silver staining) to establish lineage involvement, iron distribution, and marrow stromal fibrosis.
  • Correlate diagnostic cytogenetic and molecular biomarkers (BCR-ABL1, JAK2 V617F, CALR, MPL, del(5q), SF3B1) with morphologic classifications to substantiate disease subtyping, risk stratification, and inter-laboratory reflex pathways.

Pathophysiologic Divergence: Effective vs. Ineffective Hematopoiesis

Core Concepts & Detailed Procedure / Concept Analysis

Myeloproliferative Neoplasms (MPNs) and Myelodysplastic Syndromes (MDSs) represent clonal hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) disorders arising from acquired somatic mutations. Despite this shared origin within the multipotent hematopoietic compartment, their cellular dynamics diverge into two diametrically opposed clinical and laboratory profiles:

Myeloproliferative Neoplasms (Effective Hematopoiesis)

MPNs demonstrate autonomous, effective proliferation of one or more myeloid lineages (erythroid, granulocytic, megakaryocytic). The hematopoietic factory operates in uncontrolled "overdrive." The genetic lesions confer cytokine-independent survival and proliferation, bypassing standard endocrine and paracrine negative feedback loops. Cellular maturation proceeds to completion, releasing abundant mature elements into the circulating pool and causing peripheral leukocytosis, erythrocytosis, and/or thrombocytosis.

Myelodysplastic Syndromes (Ineffective Hematopoiesis)

MDSs are characterized by disordered differentiation, progressive cytopenias, and a high risk of transformation to Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML). The bone marrow demonstrates disordered, ineffective hematopoiesis. Precursor cells harbor lesions that induce cytoplasmic and nuclear dysplasia, impaired functional specialization, and accelerated intramedullary apoptosis. While the bone marrow space remains hypercellular or normocellular, mature cells fail to enter circulation, resulting in peripheral cytopenias (anemia, neutropenia, and/or thrombocytopenia).

Clinical Correlations, Diagnostics & QC

The benchtop Medical Laboratory Scientist (MLS) is typically the first healthcare professional to identify these clonal disorders through routine screening. Hematology automated analyzers utilize electrical impedance (Coulter principle) and optical multi-angle light scatter (forward scatter for volume/size; side scatter for internal complexity/granularity) to measure cell populations. Clonal alterations disturb normal scattergrams and impedance pulses:

  • High Takeoff Flags / Extended Granulocytic Envelopes: Signal immature granulocytes, shifting differential clusters into the immature granulocyte (IG) region.
  • Platelet-RBC Overlap: Giant platelets and fragmented microcytes or schistocytes distort the valley between the platelet (2–20 fL) and RBC (24–360 fL) histograms, triggering manual smear confirmation flags.
  • WBC Histogram Distortions: Unlysed red cells, nucleated red blood cells (nRBCs), or micromegakaryocytes generate interference at the lower leukocyte threshold (30–50 fL), necessitating corrected WBC calculations.

$$\text{Corrected WBC Count} = \text{Uncorrected WBC} \times \left(\frac{100}{100 + \text{nRBC}}\right)$$

Quality Control and Rule of Three Verification

Automated analyzers establish quality checks through the Rule of Three, which applies to normocytic, normochromic erythrocytes:

$$\text{HGB (g/dL)} \times 3 = \text{HCT (%)} \pm 3$$

$$\text{RBC (}\times 10^6/\mu\text{L)} \times 3 \approx \text{HGB (g/dL)}$$

In MPNs such as Polycythemia Vera, high cell concentrations may lead to coincident passage errors during impedance counting, necessitating manual dilution or microhematocrit validation. In MDS, marked macrocytosis, dysmorphic red cell populations, and severe anisocytosis elevate the Red Cell Distribution Width (RDW >14.5%) and invalidate simple visual Rule of Three assumptions.

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • MPNs produce hypercellular peripheral blood with elevated cell counts driven by autonomous, effective myelopoiesis.
  • MDSs demonstrate peripheral cytopenias driven by ineffective hematopoiesis, with marrow hypercellularity accompanied by premature intramedullary apoptosis.
  • An unexplained persistent cytopenia (especially macrocytic anemia with an elevated RDW) or an unexplained sustained panmyelosis is a direct laboratory trigger for a peripheral blood smear examination.

Chronic Myeloid Leukemia (CML)

  • Automated CBCD Characteristics:
    • Extreme leukocytosis: WBC frequently exceeds 50.0 $\times 10^3$/µL (50.0 $\times 10^9$/L) and often >100.0 $\times 10^3$/µL (ASCP reference range: 3.6–10.6 $\times 10^3$/µL).
    • Absolute neutrophilia, absolute eosinophilia (>0.3 $\times 10^3$/µL), and absolute basophilia (>0.2 $\times 10^3$/µL). Absolute basophilia is a major diagnostic criterion.
    • Platelet count: Elevated (>450 $\times 10^3$/µL) in roughly 50% of cases at clinical presentation.
    • Erythrocytes: Mild-to-moderate normocytic, normochromic anemia (HGB <12.0 g/dL) secondary to granulocytic expansion replacing erythroid niches.
  • Peripheral Blood Smear (PBS) Morphology:
    • Complete Granulocytic Spectrum ("Pyramid" Maturation): Granulocytes display an entire spectrum of maturation spanning myeloblasts, promyelocytes, myelocytes, metamyelocytes, bands, and segmented neutrophils. The relative cell numbers correlate with maturational maturity (segmented neutrophils and bands outnumber immature precursors).
    • "Myelocyte Bulge": A distinctive morphological characteristic wherein the myelocyte percentage exceeds that of the more mature metamyelocyte.
    • Blast Quantitation: Myeloblasts typically represent <10% of total leukocytes during the chronic phase. Peripheral or marrow blast proportions approaching or exceeding 20% mark transformation to blast phase (AML or ALL).
    • Basophilia Confirmation: Visual microscopy confirms coarse, dark-purple metachromatic granules that overlie and partially obscure the nucleus.
    • Platelet and RBC Morphology: Circulating dwarf micromegakaryocytes or fragments of megakaryocytic nuclei may be observed on the smear; erythrocytes display minimal poikilocytosis, with occasional circulating nRBCs.

Polycythemia Vera (PV)

  • Automated CBCD Characteristics:
    • Sustained erythrocytosis: Elevated RBC count (>6.00 $\times 10^6$/µL), Hemoglobin (>18.0 g/dL), and Hematocrit (>50%).
    • Panmyelosis: Accompanied by mild-to-moderate neutrophilic leukocytosis (typically 10.0–20.0 $\times 10^3$/µL), mild absolute basophilia, and thrombocytosis (>450 $\times 10^3$/µL).
  • PBS Morphology:
    • Pronounced erythrocyte crowding across the working area of the slide. Erythrocytes are typically normocytic and normochromic.
    • Microcytic, hypochromic red cells (MCV <76 fL, MCHC <32 g/dL) may emerge secondary to iron depletion following repeated therapeutic phlebotomies or occult gastrointestinal hemorrhage.
    • Neutrophils are morphologically mature with normal segmentation; an absence of prominent left-shift helps distinguish PV from CML.

Essential Thrombocythemia (ET)

  • Automated CBCD Characteristics:
    • Marked, sustained thrombocytosis: Platelet count consistently >450 $\times 10^3$/µL, regularly exceeding 1,000 $\times 10^3$/µL.
    • Erythron stability: Hemoglobin and hematocrit remain within normal reference ranges (HGB 12.0–18.0 g/dL; HCT 35–50%), a key parameter distinguishing ET from Polycythemia Vera.
    • Mild reactive-appearing neutrophilic leukocytosis in up to 50% of patients.
  • PBS Morphology:
    • Abundant platelets, presenting as large manual clumps and sheets that extend across the entire blood film.
    • Marked platelet anisocytosis: Presence of giant platelets (exceeding erythrocyte diameter, >7–8 µm), agranular forms, and irregular, elongated projections.
    • Megakaryocyte cytoplasm fragments and denuded megakaryocyte nuclei (micromegakaryocytes), typically found pushed toward the feathered edge.

Primary Myelofibrosis (PMF)

  • Automated CBCD Characteristics:
    • Highly variable counts based on disease staging: Early (prefibrotic) PMF often demonstrates striking thrombocytosis and neutrophilia, closely mimicking ET. Overt fibrotic PMF demonstrates progressive pancytopenia, marked anemia, and fluctuating leukocyte and platelet levels.
    • Analyzer Flags: Routine flagging includes Immature Granulocytes (IG), Nucleated Red Blood Cells (nRBCs), and Platelet Clumps.
  • PBS Morphology (The Classic Leukoerythroblastic Picture):
    • Leukoerythroblastosis: Circulating immature granulocytic precursors (promyelocytes, myelocytes, metamyelocytes) accompanied by circulating nucleated red blood cells, disproportionate to the severity of anemia.
    • Dacrocytes (Teardrop Cells): The morphological hallmark of PMF, formed as non-deformable erythrocytes are squeezed through distorted sinusoidal spaces within a fibrosed marrow microenvironment or during extramedullary splenic release.
    • Severe poikilocytosis and anisocytosis, often accompanied by giant, hypogranular platelets and circulating megakaryocytic nuclei.

Clinical Correlations, Diagnostics & QC

Benchtop differential diagnosis requires resolving common clinical mimics:

  • CML vs. Leukemoid Reaction: A leukemoid reaction (severe infection or inflammatory stress) triggers toxic granulation, Döhle bodies, and cytoplasmic vacuolization without an absolute basophilia or myelocyte bulge. The Leukocyte Alkaline Phosphatase (LAP) score or molecular testing for BCR-ABL1 resolves this ambiguity.
  • ET vs. Reactive Thrombocytosis: Reactive states (acute blood loss, post-splenectomy, chronic inflammatory states, iron deficiency) rarely exceed 1,000 $\times 10^3$/µL platelets and lack giant, bizarre platelets, micromegakaryocytes, or driver mutations.

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • The "Myelocyte Bulge" combined with absolute basophilia on a blood film is highly suspicious for Chronic Myeloid Leukemia until ruled out by molecular testing.
  • Polycythemia Vera is characterized by panmyelosis; an elevated hematocrit accompanied by thrombocytosis and leukocytosis indicates a primary clonal drive rather than secondary polycythemia.
  • Dacrocytes (teardrop cells) coupled with immature granulocytes and nRBCs form the classic leukoerythroblastic picture of Primary Myelofibrosis.

Dysplastic Morphologic Triad: Multi-Lineage Peripheral Dysplasia in MDS

Myelodysplastic Syndromes present across one, two, or all three myeloid lineages (dyserythropoiesis, dysgranulopoiesis, dysmegakaryopoiesis). Morphological identification must be conducted on blood smears stained with Romanowsky (Wright or Wright-Giemsa) dyes:

Dyserythropoiesis (Abnormal Erythrocytes)

  • Oval Macrocytes: Large, egg-shaped erythrocytes (macro-ovalocytes) with an increased mean corpuscular volume (MCV >100 fL, often 105–120 fL; ASCP reference range: 76–100 fL). These are morphologically distinct from the round macrocytes commonly observed in benign liver disease, obstructive jaundice, or chronic alcoholism.
  • Dimorphic Red Cell Population: The simultaneous coexistence of two distinct erythrocyte populations on a single smear (macrocytic and normocytic, or hypochromic microcytic and normochromic). This manifests as a widely broadened baseline on automated RBC histograms and an elevated Red Cell Distribution Width (RDW >14.5%; ASCP reference range: 11.5–14.5%).
  • Basophilic Stippling: Coarse, dark-blue or purple punctate granules distributed throughout the erythrocyte cytoplasm, representing pathological precipitates of aggregated ribosomal RNA resulting from impaired pyrimidine clearance.
  • Pappenheimer Bodies: True siderotic granules containing non-heme iron. On Wright-Giemsa stain, they appear as small, dense, irregular purple dots located at the cell periphery. Their iron composition is confirmed via cytochemical Perl's Prussian blue reaction.
  • Dysplastic Nucleated Red Blood Cells: When released into the peripheral circulation, nRBCs exhibit nuclear budding, multi-nucleation, internuclear bridging, and karyorrhexis (fragmentation of the condensed nucleus).

Dysgranulopoiesis (Abnormal Granulocytes)

  • Pseudo-Pelger-Huët Anomaly (Hyposegmentation): An acquired defect where mature neutrophils feature a bilobed nucleus resembling spectacles ("pince-nez") connected by a thin chromatin filament, or a completely non-segmented, peanut-shaped nucleus. The chromatin is heavily condensed and mature, distinguishing these dysplastic cells from genuine band neutrophils.
  • Hypogranulation: Granulocytes demonstrate pale or absent secondary (specific) granules, giving the cytoplasm a clear or smooth gray-blue appearance.
  • Nuclear Asynchrony and Dysmorphism: Neutrophils may show abnormal hypersegmentation (presence of $\ge$6 nuclear lobes), ring-shaped nuclei (central hole within the nuclear lobule), or disorganized chromatin clumping patterns.

Dysmegakaryopoiesis (Abnormal Platelets)

  • Giant Platelets: Circulating platelets approaching or exceeding the diameter of normal erythrocytes (diameter >7 µm).
  • Hypogranular / Agranular ("Ghost") Platelets: Platelets that lack purple $\alpha$-granules, presenting as pale, transparent, faintly basophilic structures on standard blood films.
  • Micromegakaryocytes: Circulating small, dense, rounded or bilobed bare megakaryocyte nuclei that have shed their peripheral cytoplasm.
  • Myeloblast Quantification: Circulating myeloblasts must be accurately quantified per 100 or 200 leukocytes. Peripheral myeloblasts typically remain <5% in early MDS. A finding of $\ge$5% blasts in the peripheral blood, or an increase toward 20%, signals higher-risk subclassification (MDS with excess blasts) and impending leukemic transformation.

Clinical Correlations, Diagnostics & QC

Dysplasia must be differentiated from benign mimics:

  • Pseudo-Pelger-Huët vs. Congenital Pelger-Huët: Hereditary Pelger-Huët is a benign, autosomal dominant condition in which virtually all (>90%) granulocytes demonstrate bilobed or unsegmented nuclei without hypogranulation or cytopenias. Acquired Pseudo-Pelger-Huët in MDS affects only a fraction of neutrophils and is accompanied by hypogranulation, cytopenias, and other dysplastic features.
  • Megaloblastic Anemia vs. MDS: Severe Vitamin $\text{B}_{12}$ or Folate deficiency can mimic MDS, showing pancytopenia, oval macrocytes, and hypersegmented neutrophils. However, hypogranulated neutrophils and pseudo-Pelger-Huët cells are classic features of MDS, not nutritional deficiency.

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • Acquired Pseudo-Pelger-Huët neutrophils (bilobed "pince-nez" with condensed chromatin) and hypogranular "ghost" platelets are strong morphological indicators of myelodysplasia.
  • Oval macrocytes accompanied by coarse basophilic stippling or Pappenheimer bodies warrant immediate bone marrow evaluation to assess for ring sideroblasts.
  • Any myeloblast identified in peripheral blood must be quantified and confirmed across multiple high-power fields; finding $\ge$5% blasts in peripheral blood alters both the MDS subclassification and clinical prognosis.

Bone Marrow Architecture and Cytochemical Profiling

Core Concepts & Detailed Procedure / Concept Analysis

Definitive diagnosis and World Health Organization (WHO) subclassification of MPNs and MDSs require a comprehensive bone marrow examination. The procedure yields two complementary specimens:

  • Bone Marrow Aspirate: A liquid suspension containing marrow particles and sinusoidal blood. Smears are stained with Wright-Giemsa or cytochemical agents to evaluate individual cell cytology, perform 500-cell differential counts, and determine blast percentages.
  • Bone Marrow Core Biopsy: A solid cylinder of intact trabecular bone and marrow. It is fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, decalcified, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 3–4 µm. This preserves the spatial architecture, stroma, and cell distribution.

Cellularity Estimation

Marrow cellularity is determined histologically on the core biopsy by assessing the proportion of space occupied by hematopoietic cells versus non-hematopoietic adipose tissue. The baseline expected cellularity across adults is estimated using the standard formula:

$$\text{Expected Cellularity (%)} \approx 100 - \text{Patient Age (years)} \quad (\pm 10\text{%})$$
  • MPN Cellularity: Characteristically hypercellular for age, frequently occupying 90–100% of the intratrabecular space due to expansion of the myeloid compartment.
  • MDS Cellularity: Paradoxically hypercellular or normocellular in roughly 80–90% of cases despite severe peripheral cytopenias. This discrepancy illustrates the core defect of ineffective hematopoiesis, where clonal precursors undergo intramedullary apoptosis before maturation. A minority (10–20%) present as hypocellular MDS.

Megakaryocytic Topography and Cytology

Megakaryocytes serve as a primary cell line for differentiating MPNs from MDS:

  • In ET and early PMF: Megakaryocytes are increased in number and size (large-to-giant), with abundant, mature cytoplasm and hyperlobated, deeply folded "staghorn-like" nuclei. They frequently form loose to tight clusters within core biopsy sections.
  • In CML: Megakaryocytes are often smaller than normal and possess hypolobated nuclei, but they lack overt dysplastic features.
  • In MDS: Megakaryocytes demonstrate prominent dysmegakaryopoiesis: micromegakaryocytes (dwarf forms), widely separated multi-nucleated forms, or single, non-lobated round nuclei ("pawn-ball" megakaryocytes). Rather than aggregating in clusters, they remain abnormally dispersed throughout the marrow.

Cytochemical Protocols: LAP, Prussian Blue, and Reticulin

1. Leukocyte Alkaline Phosphatase (LAP) Score

LAP activity is localized to the secondary granules of maturing neutrophils (metamyelocytes, bands, segmented neutrophils).

  • Protocol / Reaction Mechanism:
    1. Prepare fresh capillary or non-anticoagulated peripheral blood smears (EDTA can inhibit alkaline phosphatase enzyme activity; fresh smears must be fixed within hours).
    2. Fix slides in cold buffered formalin-methanol.
    3. Incubate with substrate (naphthol AS-BI phosphate) buffered to an alkaline pH. Endogenous LAP hydrolyzes the phosphate ester.
    4. The released naphthol derivative couples immediately with a diazonium salt (Fast Red Violet LB or Fast Blue BB), forming an insoluble, colored precipitate at the site of enzyme activity.
    5. Counterstain with Mayer's hematoxylin or methyl green.
    6. Score 100 consecutive bands and segmented neutrophils microscopically under oil immersion (1000$\times$). Grade each cell from 0 to 4+ based on precipitation density:
      • 0: No visible precipitate.
      • 1+: Faint, occasional cytoplasmic granules.
      • 2+: Moderate granulations throughout cytoplasm.
      • 3+: Dense, crowded granules, nucleus clearly visible.
      • 4+: Completely packed, dark cytoplasm; nucleus obscured.
    7. Calculate the total LAP score by summing the individual values (theoretical analytical range: 0–400):

$$\text{LAP Score} = \sum (\text{Cell Grade} \times \text{Number of Cells Graded})$$
  • Interpretation:
    • Low LAP Score (<20): Chronic Myeloid Leukemia (CML), Paroxysmal Nocturnal Hemoglobinuria (PNH).
    • Normal Reference Interval: ~20–130.
    • High LAP Score (>160): Leukemoid reactions, Polycythemia Vera, third-trimester pregnancy, systemic inflammatory stress.
2. Perls' Prussian Blue (Iron Stain)

Perls' Prussian blue histochemical staining detects insoluble non-heme ferric iron ($Fe^{3+}$) within marrow particles and erythroblasts.

  • Protocol / Reaction Mechanism:
    1. Fix air-dried bone marrow aspirate particle smears in pure absolute methanol.
    2. Treat slides with an equal mixture of 2% aqueous potassium ferrocyanide and 2% hydrochloric acid:

$$4\,\text{Fe}^{3+} + 3\,[\text{K}_4\text{Fe(CN)}_6] \longrightarrow \text{Fe}_4[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]_3 \downarrow (\text{Prussian Blue}) + 12\,\text{K}^+$$
3. Ferric iron ($Fe^{3+}$) uncouples from storage proteins (ferritin and hemosiderin) in the acidic medium and reacts with potassium ferrocyanide to form ferric ferrocyanide—an insoluble, bright-blue precipitate. Heme iron in hemoglobin remains unreactive.
4. Rinse with deionized water and counterstain with Nuclear Fast Red or Safranin to visualize erythroid and myeloid nuclear outlines.
  • Interpretation:
    • Macrophage Iron Stores: Evaluated under 100$\times$ and 400$\times$ magnification within marrow spicules and scored from 0 to 4+. Increased stores occur in the anemia of chronic inflammation and MDS; stores are absent in true iron deficiency anemia.
    • Normal Sideroblasts: 20–50% of late polychromatophilic and orthochromic normoblasts show 1 to 4 small, faint blue granules scattered randomly through the cytoplasm.
    • Pathological Ring Sideroblasts: Erythroid precursors containing 5 or more coarse blue iron granules encircling at least one-third of the nucleus. This pattern reflects aberrant accumulation of iron within the cristae of perinuclear mitochondria due to defective heme biosynthesis. The presence of $\ge$15% ringed sideroblasts among erythroid precursors (or $\ge$5% in the presence of a confirmed SF3B1 mutation) fulfills diagnostic criteria for MDS with Ring Sideroblasts (MDS-RS).

3. Reticulin Stain (Gomori or Gordon-Sweets Silver Impregnation)

A histopathological silver staining protocol performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded core biopsies to detect Type III collagen (reticulin fibers).

  • Protocol / Reaction Mechanism:
    1. Bone marrow core sections are treated with an oxidizing agent (potassium permanganate), followed by bleaching with oxalic acid.
    2. Slides are sensitized using iron alum or ferric ammonium sulfate.
    3. Sections are incubated in an ammoniacal silver nitrate solution. Silver ions ($Ag^+$) bind to the carbohydrate moieties of reticulin protein fibrils.
    4. A formalin reducing bath converts the bound silver ions to visible, black metallic silver precipitates ($Ag^0$).
    5. The preparation is fixed with sodium thiosulfate to remove unreduced silver, followed by a nuclear counterstain.
  • European Consensus Staging of Myelofibrosis (MF):
    • MF-0: Normal. Scattered, linear reticulin fibers without intersection (cross-linking); normal bone marrow framework.
    • MF-1: Slight increase. Loose network of intersecting reticulin fibers, mostly localized to perivascular zones.
    • MF-2: Moderate increase. Diffuse and dense network of intersecting reticulin fibers throughout the marrow spaces, with localized bundles of collagen (without osteosclerosis).
    • MF-3: Severe increase. Diffuse, coarse reticulin fiber network with extensive bundle intersections and marked coarse collagen deposition; often accompanied by new bone formation (osteosclerosis).

Clinical Correlations, Diagnostics & QC

Bone marrow testing carries several technical limitations:

  • The "Dry Tap" Dilemma: A failure to aspirate liquid marrow contents (dry tap) is a recognized clinical presentation of overt PMF, high-grade hairy cell leukemia, or packed acute leukemias. When this occurs, the bone marrow core biopsy is critical, requiring immediate reticulin and trichrome staining to evaluate for marrow fibrosis.
  • Specimen Adequacy Criteria: An adequate bone marrow core biopsy should ideally measure $\ge$1.5 cm in length and include at least 10 intact marrow intertrabecular spaces free of crushing or aspiration artifacts.
  • Biological Specificity: Reticulin deposition is a reactive, non-clonal stromal phenomenon driven by pro-fibrotic cytokines (PDGF, TGF-$\beta$) released by abnormal megakaryocytes. It is not unique to PMF and can appear in advanced PV, ET, metastatic carcinoma, and systemic inflammatory disorders.

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • LAP scores <20 point toward Chronic Myeloid Leukemia, whereas leukemoid reactions typically yield scores >160.
  • A ring sideroblast requires $\ge$5 iron granules encircling $\ge$ one-third of the erythroid precursor's nucleus, representing iron-trapped mitochondria.
  • A bone marrow aspirate "dry tap" should raise suspicion for overt reticulin fibrosis (PMF MF-2/MF-3) or packed marrow infiltration, shifting diagnostic focus to the core biopsy.

Conventional Cytogenetics and Karyotyping

  • Principle: Viable, dividing hematopoietic cells from a bone marrow aspirate are cultured with mitogens, arrested in metaphase using colchicine/colcemid, treated with hypotonic potassium chloride (KCl) solution to swell cells and disperse chromatin, and fixed with Carnoy's fixative (3:1 methanol:glacial acetic acid). Suspensions are dropped onto slides and stained with Giemsa (G-banding). A laboratory scientist analyzes a minimum of 20 banded metaphase spreads to construct a diagnostic karyogram.

  • Diagnostic Utility in MPNs:

    • Primary application: Detection of the Philadelphia chromosome, a reciprocal translocation between the long arms of chromosomes 9 and 22:

    $$\text{t}(9;22)(\text{q}34.1;\text{q}11.2)$$

    This structural alteration moves the ABL1 proto-oncogene on chromosome 9 to the BCR region on chromosome 22, yielding an abbreviated derivative chromosome 22 (the Philadelphia chromosome) diagnostic for CML.

  • Diagnostic Utility in MDS:

    • Karyotyping is useful for subtyping and prognostic stratification (e.g., the Revised International Prognostic Scoring System, IPSS-R). Approximately 50% of primary MDS cases present with a normal karyotype (46,XX or 46,XY).
    • Recurring unbalanced structural and numerical abnormalities in MDS include:
    • Deletion of 5q [del(5q)]: Associated with the favorable 5q- syndrome, characterized by macrocytic anemia and hypolobated megakaryocytes.
    • Monosomy 7 (-7) or deletion of 7q [del(7q)]: Associated with high risk of AML transformation and poor prognosis.
    • Trisomy 8 (+8): Intermediate cytogenetic risk group.
    • Deletion of 20q [del(20q)]: Favorable cytogenetic category when isolated.
  • Inter-Laboratory Procedure: Refer to the Cytogenetics Laboratory.

Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR) for BCR-ABL1

  • Principle: Total RNA is isolated from peripheral blood leukocytes or bone marrow aspirates and transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using reverse transcriptase. Sequence-specific primers flanking the junction of the BCR and ABL1 fusion site initiate polymerase amplification. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) monitors amplification in real time using fluorogenic hydrolysis probes.
  • Clinical Utility:
    • Resolves cryptic t(9;22) translocations that cannot be detected by conventional 20-metaphase G-banded karyotyping.
    • Serves as the quantitative standard of care for monitoring minimal residual disease (MRD) during Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitor (TKI) therapy to verify a Major Molecular Response (MMR).
  • Inter-Laboratory Procedure: Refer to the Molecular Diagnostics Laboratory.

  • JAK2 V617F: A gain-of-function somatic point mutation in exon 14 of the Janus kinase 2 gene on chromosome 9p, resulting in a valine-to-phenylalanine substitution at codon 617. Detected via allele-specific PCR or pyrosequencing.
    • Present in ~95% of Polycythemia Vera cases (with the remainder typically harboring mutations in JAK2 exon 12).

  • CALR (Calreticulin): Insertion or deletion mutations within exon 9 of the calreticulin gene, detected by PCR fragment analysis and capillary electrophoresis.
    • Present in 20–25% of ET and PMF cases. Associated with higher platelet counts and lower thrombosis risk relative to JAK2-mutated cases.

Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) Panels

  • Principle: High-throughput massively parallel sequencing that analyzes targeted DNA panels covering dozens of recurring genes associated with myeloid malignancies.
  • Diagnostic Utility in MDS:
    • Clonal Detection in Karyotypically Normal Disease: Confirms a clonal neoplastic process in patients with unexplained cytopenias and equivocal morphological dysplasia who have a normal cytogenetic karyotype (46,XX or 46,XY).
    • Recurring Somatic Targets:
    • RNA Splicing Machinery: SF3B1 (strongly correlated with ring sideroblasts, favorable prognosis), SRSF2, U2AF1.
    • Epigenetic Regulation & DNA Methylation: TET2, DNMT3A, ASXL1 (associated with aggressive disease and poor prognosis).
    • Transcription Factors: RUNX1.
    • Tumor Suppressors: TP53 mutations (associated with multi-complex karyotypes, resistance to standard chemotherapy, and high rates of transformation to AML).
  • Diagnostic Utility in MPNs:
    • Identifies underlying mutations in "triple-negative" ET and PMF cases.
    • Detects secondary "high-molecular-risk" mutations (ASXL1, SRSF2, IDH1/IDH2) that may indicate disease transformation or resistance to therapy.
  • Inter-Laboratory Procedure: Refer to the Molecular Diagnostics Laboratory or Reference Laboratory.

Flow Cytometry Immunophenotyping

  • Principle: Laser-based detection of cell suspensions incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies directed against Cluster of Differentiation (CD) antigens. Hydrodynamic focusing forces cells single-file through an interrogating laser beam; forward light scatter measures cell volume, side light scatter assesses internal complexity, and fluorophore emissions identify cell-surface and intracellular immunophenotypes.
  • Application in MDS:
    • Provides objective phenotypic evidence of dysplasia to supplement morphology. Dysplastic patterns include aberrant loss of lineage-specific antigens (loss of CD13 or CD33 on granulocytes), asynchronous antigen co-expression (simultaneous expression of early CD34 with mature CD15), and cross-lineage expression (aberrant presentation of lymphoid markers such as CD7 on CD34+ myeloblasts).
    • Delivers accurate quantitation of the marrow blast population via CD34, CD117, and CD45 expression profiles.
  • Inter-Laboratory Procedure: Refer to the Flow Cytometry Laboratory.

Clinical Correlations, Diagnostics & QC

Molecular and cytogenetic investigations must be integrated with the clinical history and morphologic assessment. Finding a somatic mutation via NGS does not necessarily indicate MDS or MPN on its own, as age-related clonal hematopoiesis can occur without clinical disease. Similarly, testing negative for JAK2, CALR, and MPL does not fully rule out ET or PMF. Diagnoses require correlating CBCD patterns, peripheral blood and bone marrow morphology, cytochemical staining, and molecular findings.

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • Detection of the t(9;22)(q34.1;q11.2) translocation or its molecular product, BCR-ABL1, is required to diagnose Chronic Myeloid Leukemia and differentiate it from other MPNs or leukemoid reactions.
  • Polycythemia Vera is linked to JAK2 mutations in >98% of cases (~95% in exon 14 V617F; remainder in exon 12); a normal JAK2 assay strongly points toward secondary polycythemia.
  • CALR and MPL driver mutations serve as alternate diagnostic markers in roughly 30% of ET and PMF cases lacking JAK2 mutations.
  • The presence of an SF3B1 mutation in MDS strongly correlates with the formation of ring sideroblasts and a more favorable clinical prognosis.
