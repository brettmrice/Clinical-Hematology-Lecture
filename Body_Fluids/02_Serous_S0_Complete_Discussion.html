Serous Fluids

Learning Objectives

  • Differentiate the physiological mechanisms of serous fluid formation and classify effusions as transudates or exudates using standard clinical laboratory criteria (Light's criteria and the Serum-Ascites Albumin Gradient).
  • Outline specimen collection protocols, tube additive requirements, and pre-analytical preservation strategies across pleural, pericardial, and peritoneal fluids.
  • Evaluate physical and chemical examination parameters of serous fluids to correlate gross appearance and biochemical markers with systemic versus localized disease etiologies.
  • Distinguish benign, reactive, and malignant cellular morphology on stained cytocentrifuge preparations using standard cytological criteria.
  • Calculate manual hemocytometer total nucleated cell and red blood cell counts while troubleshooting pre-analytical and analytical specimen interferences.

Physiological Formation and Effusion Classification

Core Concepts & Detailed Procedure / Concept Analysis

Serous cavities—the pleural, pericardial, and peritoneal cavities—are closed anatomical spaces lined by a continuous serous membrane composed of a single layer of flattened mesothelial cells supported by a thin subserosal stroma containing blood and lymphatic capillaries.

The membrane is divided into two continuous anatomical layers:

  • Parietal Membrane: Lines the outer cavity wall. Its microvasculature is supplied by systemic arterioles with higher hydrostatic capillary pressure.
  • Visceral Membrane: Covers the internal organs. Its venous drainage typically connects to low-pressure venous circulation.

Under normal physiological conditions, serous fluid functions as a biological ultrafiltrate of plasma that continuously lubricates opposing membrane surfaces to minimize mechanical friction. Normal serous fluid volume is maintained at a dynamic steady state governed by Starling forces:

$$\text{Net Filtration Pressure} = (P_c - P_i) - \sigma(\pi_c - \pi_i)$$

Where:

  • $P_c$ is capillary hydrostatic pressure
  • $P_i$ is interstitial/cavity hydrostatic pressure
  • $\sigma$ is the osmotic reflection coefficient for plasma proteins
  • $\pi_c$ is capillary plasma colloid osmotic pressure (oncotic pressure mediated by albumin)
  • $\pi_i$ is cavity fluid oncotic pressure

An abnormal accumulation of serous fluid within a cavity is designated an effusion. Effusions are fundamentally classified into two pathophysiological categories:

  1. Transudates:

    • Formed secondary to systemic disruptions in fluid filtration or reabsorption dynamics without intrinsic disease of the serous membranes.
    • Pathophysiological mechanisms include:
      • Elevated capillary hydrostatic pressure (e.g., congestive heart failure).
      • Markedly decreased plasma oncotic pressure due to hypoalbuminemia (e.g., nephrotic syndrome, end-stage liver disease).
    • Capillary permeability remains normal; high-molecular-weight proteins and cellular elements are retained in circulation.

  2. Exudates:

    • Formed secondary to direct, localized inflammation, infection, vascular damage, or malignancy involving the serous membranes.
    • Endothelial junctions widen, resulting in marked capillary hyperpermeability. High-molecular-weight plasma proteins, lactate dehydrogenase (LD), and circulating inflammatory leukocytes pour into the serous cavity.
    • Decreased local lymphatic clearance due to tumor involvement or mechanical obstruction further exacerbates fluid pooling.

Light's Criteria (Gold Standard for Pleural Fluid)

To differentiate transudates from exudates in pleural fluid, paired serous fluid and peripheral serum samples must be collected and analyzed simultaneously. An effusion is classified as an exudate if it meets one or more of the following three criteria:

  • Fluid-to-Serum Total Protein Ratio: $> 0.5$
  • Fluid-to-Serum Lactate Dehydrogenase (LD) Ratio: $> 0.6$
  • Fluid LD Activity: $> 2/3$ ($> 67\text{%}$) of the upper limit of normal (ULN) for serum LD

If the fluid fails to meet any of the three criteria, it is confirmed as a transudate.

Serum-Ascites Albumin Gradient (SAAG) (Gold Standard for Peritoneal Fluid)

For peritoneal fluid (ascites), Light's criteria lack optimal specificity, particularly in patients on diuretic therapy or with underlying cirrhosis. The Serum-Ascites Albumin Gradient (SAAG) is the superior diagnostic standard:

$$\text{SAAG} = \text{Serum Albumin (g/dL)} - \text{Fluid Albumin (g/dL)}$$
  • High Gradient ($\text{SAAG} \ge 1.1\text{ g/dL}$): Indicates the presence of portal hypertension driving transudation (increased sinusoidal hydrostatic pressure). Classic etiologies: cirrhosis, congestive heart failure, Budd-Chiari syndrome.
  • Low Gradient ($\text{SAAG} < 1.1\text{ g/dL}$): Indicates the absence of portal hypertension; fluid formation is driven by direct peritoneal membrane disease and hyperpermeability. Classic etiologies: peritoneal carcinomatosis, peritoneal tuberculosis, nephrotic syndrome, pancreatitis.

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • Transudates stem from non-membrane systemic disorders; exudates stem from direct inflammation, infection, or malignancy of the cavity lining.
  • Light's criteria require only one positive threshold to designate pleural fluid as an exudate.
  • Spontaneous in vitro clot formation indicates the presence of fibrinogen, directly identifying the fluid as an exudate or indicating peripheral blood contamination from a traumatic procedure.
  • For peritoneal effusions, a $\text{SAAG} \ge 1.1\text{ g/dL}$ establishes portal hypertension as the driving force behind fluid accumulation.

Core Concepts & Detailed Procedure / Concept Analysis

Serous fluids are invasive collections requiring direct needle aspiration by a qualified physician. Specimen preservation begins immediately upon puncture.

  • Thoracentesis: Aspiration of pleural fluid from the thoracic pleural space.
  • Pericardiocentesis: Aspiration of pericardial fluid from the pericardial sac.
  • Paracentesis: Aspiration of peritoneal (ascitic) fluid from the abdominopelvic cavity.

Multi-Tube Triaging and Anticoagulant Requirements

Because serous fluids often contain fibrinogen (especially exudates), unanticoagulated specimens can rapidly form micro-clots, entrapping nucleated cells and erythrocytes. Specimen collection requires systematic aliquoting:

  1. EDTA Tube (Lavender Top):
    • Target Section: Hematology (Manual Hemocytometer Cell Counts, Microscopic Cytospin Differential).
    • Mechanism: Chelation of divalent calcium ions ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$) prevents cascade activation and fibrin clot formation, preserving cells in suspension without altering staining morphology.
  2. Heparinized (Green Top) or Plain Red Top (No Additive) Tube:
    • Target Section: Clinical Chemistry and Immunology/Serology (Total Protein, Albumin, LD, Glucose, Amylase, Lipids, Tumor Markers).
    • Note: Plain red-top tubes allow complete clotting if the fluid is an exudate; centrifugation separates fluid serum for chemistry testing. Sodium heparin is the preferred anticoagulant if uncentrifuged non-clotted fluid is required for biochemical testing.
  3. Sterile Tube / Direct Inoculation Media:
    • Target Section: Clinical Microbiology (Gram stain, acid-fast bacillus [AFB] stain, aerobic and anaerobic cultures, fungal cultures).
    • Note: Fluid may be directly inoculated into pediatric or adult blood culture bottles at the bedside to increase the diagnostic yield of microorganisms.

Pre-Analytical Stability and Storage Rules

  • Processing Timeframe: All cell counts and differentials must be performed within 60 minutes of collection.
  • Cellular Degradation: Mesothelial cells and polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) degenerate rapidly at room temperature. Neutrophils undergo apoptosis, cellular swelling, and karyorrhexis, hindering differentiation from small lymphocytes or necrotic debris. Mesothelial cells develop cytoplasmic vacuolization and nuclear disintegration that mimics degenerative malignancy.
  • In Vitro Glycolysis: Living leukocytes and bacteria metabolize glucose in unseparated tubes. A delay exceeding 1 hour falsely decreases fluid glucose and falsely elevates fluid lactate.
  • Storage Protocols:
    • EDTA and Chemistry Tubes: Maintain at $2\text{--}8^\circ\text{C}$ if analysis cannot be completed within 1 hour.
    • Microbiology Specimens: Maintain at room temperature ($20\text{--}25^\circ\text{C}$). Refrigeration kills fastidious bacteria such as Streptococcus pneumoniae or Neisseria species.

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • Serous fluids are irreplaceable; do not discard any fraction without consulting supervisory staff or the attending clinician.
  • Always assess the EDTA tube for micro-clots before performing cell counts. If a clot is present, cell counts are falsely decreased, and this pre-analytical compromise must be documented.
  • Never refrigerate tubes designated for bacterial recovery; maintain at room temperature to preserve fastidious organisms.
  • Fluid glucose drops and LD rises if the specimen sits uncentrifuged at room temperature due to ongoing leukocyte and bacterial metabolic activity.

Macroscopic Color and Clarity

  • Normal / Uncomplicated Transudate: Pale yellow, straw-colored, and crystal clear.
  • Cloudy / Turbid: Indicative of elevated nucleated cells, intracellular lipid debris, or microorganisms. Frank pus indicates an empyema in the pleural space or spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) in the peritoneal cavity.
  • Milky / Opalescent: Indicates high lipid content. The fluid must be differentiated into true chylous versus pseudochylous effusions:
    • Chylous Effusion: Caused by disruption, rupture, or malignant obstruction of the thoracic duct or major lymphatic channels. Characterized by high concentrations of chylomicrons and triglycerides ($> 110\text{ mg/dL}$). Following centrifugation, the supernatant remains persistently milky.
    • Pseudochylous Effusion: Caused by chronic cellular breakdown and lysis in longstanding, chronic inflammatory effusions (e.g., chronic rheumatoid pleuritis, tuberculosis). Characterized by high concentrations of cellular cholesterol crystals and low triglycerides ($< 50\text{ mg/dL}$). Following centrifugation, the supernatant typically clears as the cellular debris pellets.
  • Bloody / Hemorrhagic: Presence of red blood cells. Requires rapid differentiation between a traumatic needle aspiration versus true in vivo internal hemorrhage (e.g., trauma, pulmonary infarction, malignancy):
    • Traumatic Tap: Clearing of blood intensity during aspiration into successive collection containers; specimen frequently forms irregular clots due to the sudden introduction of fresh tissue tissue factor and platelets into normal fluid. Centrifuged supernatant is clear/straw-colored.
    • True Hemorrhage (e.g., Hemothorax): Uniformly bloody throughout the entire aspiration; fluid does not form fresh clots (the blood has already defibrinated in the cavity over time). Centrifuged supernatant is often xanthochromic.
    • Fluid Hematocrit Threshold: A pleural fluid hematocrit $> 50\text{%}$ of the patient's peripheral blood hematocrit confirms a hemothorax, requiring urgent surgical intervention.
  • Green / Dark Brown: Reflects biliverdin and bilirubin pigments, confirming biliary system perforation, gallbladder necrosis, or duodenal ulcer rupture.

Specialized Chemistry Biomarkers

  • Glucose:
    • Normal levels parallel peripheral fasting serum glucose ($74\text{--}100\text{ mg/dL}$).
    • Markedly decreased fluid levels ($< 60\text{ mg/dL}$, or a fluid-to-serum ratio $< 0.5$) indicate active consumption by bacteria (e.g., empyema), extensive leukocytic phagocytosis, or rheumatoid pleuritis. Rheumatoid effusions frequently yield glucose levels $< 30\text{ mg/dL}$.
  • Amylase:
    • Pleural fluid amylase levels exceeding the serum upper reference limit indicate acute pancreatitis with transdiaphragmatic lymphatic communication, pancreatic pseudocyst, or esophageal rupture (salivary amylase entering the left pleural cavity).
  • Triglycerides and Cholesterol:
    • Fluid triglyceride $> 110\text{ mg/dL}$ establishes a thoracic duct disruption (chylous effusion).
    • Fluid triglyceride $< 50\text{ mg/dL}$ with elevated fluid cholesterol points to a pseudochylous process.
  • Adenosine Deaminase (ADA):
    • ADA activity $> 40\text{ U/L}$ in pleural fluid has high sensitivity and specificity for tuberculous pleuritis.
  • pH:
    • Pleural fluid $\text{pH} < 7.20$ in a parapneumonic effusion indicates an evolving, complicated empyema requiring invasive chest tube drainage. A $\text{pH} < 6.00$ indicates esophageal rupture. Fluid for pH testing must be collected anaerobically in a heparinized syringe on wet ice.

Clinical Correlations, Diagnostics & QC

Laboratory validation of chemical analytes in serous fluid requires understanding analyzer limitations. Automated chemistry platforms are validated primarily for human serum or plasma. Testing serous fluids constitutes an off-label (laboratory-developed) application requiring in-house matrix validation for analytical accuracy, precision, and reportable ranges.

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • Milky fluid that remains turbid after centrifugation is chylous; quantify triglycerides ($> 110\text{ mg/dL}$ confirms).
  • A pleural fluid hematocrit value $> 50\text{%}$ of the circulating blood hematocrit establishes a diagnosis of hemothorax, distinguishing it from simple hemorrhagic exudate.
  • Low glucose ($< 60\text{ mg/dL}$) combined with elevated LD ($> 3\times$ normal) and high PMNs indicates an active infectious process (empyema/bacterial peritonitis) until proven otherwise.
  • Elevated pleural fluid amylase isolates the pathology below the diaphragm (pancreatitis) or within the posterior mediastinum (esophageal perforation).

Cytocentrifugation Technique

Direct smear preparations from unconcentrated serous fluid frequently result in cell distortion and low cellular density. A cytocentrifuge concentrates cells into an uncrowded monolayer on a delineated circle on the slide while absorbing fluid supernatant into filter cards.

  • Artifact Prevention: Adding a drop of 22% or 30% bovine serum albumin (BSA) preserves fragile cellular membranes and minimizes mechanical distortion or cytoplasmic disruption during high-speed centrifugation.

Normal Cellular Elements

  • Mesothelial Cells:
    • Normal lining cells of the pleural, pericardial, and peritoneal cavities.
    • Morphological features: Large cells ($15\text{--}30\ \mu\text{m}$), round to oval eccentric nucleus with smooth, uniform nuclear chromatin, a distinct non-cleaved nuclear membrane, and abundant, medium-blue cytoplasm.
    • Cell-to-Cell "Windowing": Mesothelial cells often shed in monolayered flat sheets. Intercellular spaces between contiguous mesothelial cells appear as clear slits or "windows" produced by interdigitating surface microvilli. This is a critical hallmark of benignity.
  • Macrophage/Monocyte Lineage:
    • Variable size ($15\text{--}80\ \mu\text{m}$) containing indented, reniform, or folded nuclei, granular or lacy chromatin, and abundant pale blue-gray cytoplasm with fine vacuoles.
    • Phagocytic phenotypes reflect underlying cavity conditions:
    • Lipophages: Lipid-laden foamy macrophages seen in chylous effusions.
    • Siderophages: Hemosiderin-pigmented macrophages containing blue-brown cytoplasmic granules from past erythrocyte phagocytosis, confirming prior internal hemorrhage.
  • Neutrophils, Lymphocytes, and Eosinophils:
    • Normal transudates contain scattered small, mature lymphocytes and resting monocytes.

Abnormal and Reactive Cytology

  • Reactive Mesothelial Cells:
    • In response to physical friction, trauma, or infection, mesothelial cells proliferate and transform.
    • Features: Cell enlargement, multinucleation (2–3 identical nuclei), intensely basophilic cytoplasm, and prominent nucleoli.
    • Crucially, reactive mesothelial cells maintain smooth, uniform nuclear membranes, an open chromatin distribution, regular spacing, and visible intercellular windows.
  • Malignant Cells (Metastatic Adenocarcinoma and Primary Mesothelioma):
    • Presence in serous fluid indicates stage IV disseminated disease or primary serosal malignancy.
    • Morphological criteria for malignancy:
    • Three-dimensional cell balls: Dense, cohesive clusters lacking intercellular windows.
    • Cannibalism / Phagocytosis: One malignant cell engulfing another.
    • Nuclear Atypia: Marked pleomorphism, high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic (N:C) ratio, hyperchromatic clumped chromatin, sharp angular nuclear notches/indentations, and giant, irregularly shaped macronucleoli.
    • Cytoplasmic Mucin Vacuoles: Signet-ring morphology seen in metastatic gastric or lobular breast carcinoma.
  • Lupus Erythematosus (LE) Cells:
    • Neutrophils that have engulfed an amorphous, round, denatured nuclear protein mass (LE body). Characteristic of pleural involvement in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

Clinical Correlations, Diagnostics & QC

Distinguishing highly reactive mesothelial cells from metastatic adenocarcinoma is one of the most challenging morphological tasks in the clinical laboratory.

  • Analytical Pitfall: Never classify cell clusters as malignant on an automated instrument or based solely on nucleolar prominence.
  • Quality Control Mandate: Any serous fluid displaying unexplained cell clustering, macronucleoli, cannibalism, or an atypical monomorphic lymphocyte population must be referred to a senior pathologist/cytotechnologist for review.
  • Confirmatory Testing: Cell blocks are prepared from the residual fluid pellet, fixed in formalin, and processed for immunohistochemical (IHC) profiling:
    • Adenocarcinoma Profile: Positive for EpCAM (MOC-31), Claudin-4, CEA, and Ber-EP4; negative for mesothelial markers.
    • Mesothelial Profile (Benign or Mesothelioma): Positive for Calretinin, WT-1, Cytokeratin 5/6, and Podoplanin (D2-40); negative for Claudin-4.

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • Intercellular "windowing" (slit-like spaces between contiguous cells) strongly supports a benign mesothelial origin.
  • Three-dimensional irregular cell balls, marked nuclear pleomorphism, and cellular cannibalism point to a malignant effusion.
  • Lymphocytic predominance ($> 50\text{%}$) in pleural fluid warrants immediate evaluation for tuberculosis (ADA testing) or underlying lymphoma.
  • Eosinophilia ($> 10\text{%}$) in pleural fluid is frequently secondary to repeated thoracentesis, introduction of air (pneumothorax), or blood in the cavity.

The Improved Neubauer Hemocytometer Standard Calculation

Manual calculations adhere strictly to the standardized formula:

$$\text{Cells}/\mu\text{L} = \frac{\text{Total Cells Counted} \times \text{Dilution Factor}}{\text{Number of Large Squares Counted} \times 0.1\text{ mm (Depth)}}$$

Where:

  • The counting area is composed of 9 large corner/center squares, each with an area of $1.0\text{ mm}^2$.
  • The fixed chamber depth between the coverslip and counting grid platform is $0.1\text{ mm}$.
  • Therefore, the volume of one large square is $1.0\text{ mm}^2 \times 0.1\text{ mm} = 0.1\text{ mm}^3 = 0.1\ \mu\text{L}$.

Step-by-Step Practical Calculation Example

A turbid pleural fluid specimen is diluted $1:10$ with $3\text{%}$ glacial acetic acid to lyse red blood cells. The technician counts nucleated cells across the 4 large corner squares on both sides of the hemocytometer chamber (total 8 squares counted):

  • Side A (4 corner squares): 120 cells
  • Side B (4 corner squares): 128 cells
  • Total cells counted across both grids = $248\text{ cells}$
  • Total large squares counted = $8\text{ squares}$
  • Dilution factor = $10$

$$\text{Cells}/\mu\text{L} = \frac{248 \times 10}{8 \times 0.1} = \frac{2,480}{0.8} = 3,100\ \text{Total Nucleated Cells}/\mu\text{L}$$

This elevated cell count ($> 1,000/\mu\text{L}$) confirms the effusion is an exudate.

Clinical Correlations, Diagnostics & QC

Chamber Count Quality Control Rules

  • Bilateral Agreement: When counting both platforms of the Neubauer hemocytometer (Side A and Side B), the total cell counts between the two chambers should agree within $10\text{--}15\text{%}$. Disagreement exceeding $15\text{%}$ indicates non-uniform charging, air bubbles, coverslip displacement, or particulate contamination; the chamber must be cleaned and recharged.
  • Humidified Settling: The charged hemocytometer must rest undisturbed for 3 to 5 minutes inside a covered Petri dish containing a moistened filter paper. This allows cells to settle onto the ruling plane without evaporative drying that alters volume and cell concentrations.

Interferences and Troubleshooting Protocols

  • Presence of Micro-clots: Incomplete mixing with EDTA or specimen collection in plain tubes allows fibrin formation. Never pull out or discard a clot and run the remaining liquid; millions of cells are trapped within the fibrin lattice, falsely depressing the WBC count. The specimen must be rejected for cell enumeration.
  • Traumatic Tap vs. Hemorrhage: If significant blood is present, red cells can obscure nucleated cells. Dilution with $3\text{%}$ glacial acetic acid or commercial WBC-lysing diluents selectively lyses anucleated red blood cells while leaving nucleated white cells and mesothelial cells intact for counting.
  • Debris vs. Small Cells: Non-cellular protein precipitate can mimic small lymphocytes. Adding a small amount of basic stain (e.g., new methylene blue) to the diluent aids in distinguishing viable nucleated cells from anuclear protein debris under brightfield illumination.

Key Takeaways & Diagnostic Pearls

  • The standard manual hemocytometer equation denominator is: $\text{Squares Counted} \times 0.1\text{ mm}$ (or simply $\text{Volume in }\mu\text{L}$).
  • Use $3\text{%}$ glacial acetic acid whenever red blood cells are dense enough to overlap, obscure, or confuse nucleated cell counts.
  • Never attempt a chamber count on a clotted fluid specimen; the resulting counts are falsely low.
  • If chamber counts between Side A and Side B diverge by more than $15\text{%}$, clean, remix, recharge, and repeat the count.

ASCP MLS Content Guideline Alignment

Urinalysis and Body Fluids

  • Body Fluids (e.g., CSF, Amniotic, Synovial, Serous, Semen, Feces)
    • Physiology
      • Fluid formation via Starling forces across the parietal membrane and reabsorption through the visceral serosal membranes and lymphatic channels.
      • Pathophysiological mechanisms of effusions: transudates (systemic hydrostatic elevation, oncotic reduction) versus exudates (localized membrane hyperpermeability, inflammatory and malignant processes).
      • Anatomical fluid dynamics of pleural, pericardial, and peritoneal (ascites) spaces.
    • Physical
      • Correlation of fluid clarity (clear, hazy, cloudy, purulent/empyema) with cell counts and microbiology.
      • Gross color interpretation: pale yellow (normal/transudate), hemorrhagic/bloody (traumatic tap versus true internal hemothorax/malignancy), green/brown (biliary rupture), and opalescent/milky (chylous versus pseudochylous effusions).
      • Post-centrifugation supernatant properties: persistent lipid turbidity in chylous effusions versus pelleting clearing in pseudochylous processes.
      • Evaluation of spontaneous clot formation driven by extravasated fibrinogen in exudates.
    • Chemical
      • Application of Light's criteria for pleural fluid classification:
        • Pleural fluid to serum total protein ratio $> 0.5$
        • Pleural fluid to serum LD ratio $> 0.6$
        • Pleural fluid LD activity $> 2/3$ upper limit of normal for serum LD
      • Calculation and clinical interpretation of the Serum-Ascites Albumin Gradient ($\text{SAAG} = \text{Serum Albumin} - \text{Fluid Albumin}$):
        • High gradient ($\ge 1.1\text{ g/dL}$) indicating portal hypertension (cirrhosis, CHF).
        • Low gradient ($< 1.1\text{ g/dL}$) indicating primary peritoneal pathology (carcinomatosis, peritonitis).
      • Biomarker analysis:
        • Glucose consumption ($< 60\text{ mg/dL}$) in empyema, bacterial peritonitis, and rheumatoid pleurisy.
        • Amylase elevation in acute pancreatitis, pseudocysts, and esophageal perforation.
        • Triglycerides ($> 110\text{ mg/dL}$) as the diagnostic marker for thoracic duct disruption.
        • Adenosine deaminase (ADA $> 40\text{ U/L}$) as a diagnostic marker for serosal tuberculosis.
        • Fluid pH ($< 7.20$) indicating complicated parapneumonic effusions requiring chest tube placement.
    • Microscopic
      • Manual hemocytometer chamber calculation using the standard formula:
      • Acetic acid ($3\text{%}$) RBC lysis technique for bloody body fluids.
      • Cytocentrifugation preparation and Wright-Giemsa staining protocols for 100-cell differentials.
      • Morphological discrimination between benign lining mesothelial cells, reactive mesothelial cells (intercellular windows, smooth nuclear envelope, nucleoli), and malignant adenocarcinoma/mesothelioma cells (3D clusters, nuclear pleomorphism, cannibalism, abnormal N:C).
      • Identification of inflammatory elements: neutrophilic empyema, lymphocytic tuberculosis/lymphoma, and serosal eosinophilia.
      • Identification of lipid-laden macrophages (lipophages), hemosiderin-laden macrophages (siderophages), and lupus erythematosus (LE) cells.
    • Disease States
      • Congestive heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, and hepatic cirrhosis presenting as transudates.
      • Bacterial pneumonia, empyema, and spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) presenting as acute neutrophilic exudates.
      • Disseminated metastatic carcinomatosis, malignant mesothelioma, and lymphoma presenting as malignant exudates.
      • Chronic rheumatoid arthritis and tuberculous serositis presenting as chronic inflammatory exudates.

Chemistry

  • Proteins and Enzymes
    • Spectrophotometric quantification of total protein and albumin in serous fluids and concurrent serum specimens.
    • Measurement of lactate dehydrogenase (LD) activity to assess serosal cellular destruction.
  • Calculations
    • Calculation of fluid-to-serum protein and LD ratios for Light's criteria.
    • Calculation of the Serum-Ascites Albumin Gradient (SAAG).

Hematology

  • Cell Counting and Microscopic Examination
    • Standard improved Neubauer hemocytometer chamber counting principles and depth-volume mathematics.
    • Technical utilization of bovine serum albumin (BSA) in cytocentrifugation to prevent artifactual cellular disruption during preparation.

Microbiology

  • Specimen Processing and Direct Examination
    • Immediate room-temperature transport and preservation protocols for body fluid culture specimens.
    • Microscopic evaluation of direct Gram stain smears on cytocentrifuged serous fluid deposits.
    • Correlation of microbial recovery (e.g., Streptococcus pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, enteric Gram-negative bacilli) with low glucose, high LD, and high neutrophil counts.

Laboratory Operations

  • Pre-Analytical Specimen Triaging and Handling
    • Tube order of fill and specific anticoagulant mechanisms: EDTA (lavender) for cellular enumeration and morphology, heparin/plain red for chemistry and serology, sterile containers for microbiology.
    • STAT operational mandate: Processing cell counts and microscopic preparations within 60 minutes of collection to avoid in vitro leukocyte degeneration and metabolic glycolysis.
    • Specimen rejection criteria: Rejection of clotted EDTA samples for cell counting due to cell entrapment.
